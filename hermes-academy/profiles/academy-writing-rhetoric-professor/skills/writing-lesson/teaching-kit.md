# Human teaching examples: Support a claim without overstating evidence

These synthetic examples are optional scaffolding, not a required lesson script. Adapt to the learner's objective and level. They establish no real-world current facts, credentials, or professional recommendations.

## Diagnostic and misconception

A fictional library survey has 20 voluntary replies, 15 favor later hours. Assess: Everyone wants the library open late.

Watch for: Treating a self-selected sample as everyone; polishing wording before fixing scope.

## Worked example

A defensible revision is: Fifteen of 20 respondents favored later hours; a broader survey is needed before inferring community demand. The revision keeps the result but limits the population claim.

## Hint ladder

Reveal one hint at a time only when needed; do not give all hints with a transfer task.

1. Who actually answered?
2. Replace everyone with the observed group.
3. Separate the observed count from the wider conclusion.

## Different transfer task

A fictional club receives eight complaints after changing its meeting day. Draft one warranted claim and one uncertainty.

Do not expose the worked solution or feedback key before the learner attempts it, unless they explicitly request a direct answer.

## Feedback criteria

Credit accurate respondent counts and scope; correct only unsupported generalization. Different wording with the same warrant is valid.

Name the learner's correct reasoning first, then the smallest concrete correction. Ask for only the changed part if a retry is useful.

## Direct answer and demonstrated understanding

For a direct question: A warrant connects evidence to a claim; its adequacy depends on what the evidence can support.

When the learner already understands: Review my argument; I distinguish the 15 respondents from the whole community and acknowledge self-selection. Assess the submitted reasoning; confirm correct work without adding obligatory practice. If no attempt is supplied, do not claim mastery.
