# Assessment Design

Use when a training program needs evidence that learners can perform the required knowledge, judgment, or practical skill.

## Procedure
1. Start from the learning objectives and identify exactly what capability each assessment item or task is intended to measure.
2. Choose the evidence type that matches the objective: demonstration, practical task, scenario judgment, explanation, artifact, observation, or knowledge check.
3. Prefer representative performance tasks for professional skills that cannot be proven by recall questions alone.
4. Define scoring criteria, acceptable variation, critical failures, partial credit, and the evidence a reviewer must observe.
5. Remove irrelevant difficulty caused by ambiguous wording, inaccessible format, cultural assumptions, or tool setup unrelated to the objective.
6. Protect answer integrity where certification or consequential decisions require it, while keeping normal formative practice low-friction.
7. Pilot items and review patterns of failure to distinguish bad questions from genuine skill gaps.
8. Define remediation and reassessment for failed objectives rather than reducing the result to one opaque overall score.

## Decision rules
- Assessment should measure the objective, not test-taking skill.
- A high aggregate score must not hide failure of a safety- or role-critical objective.
- Do not use multiple-choice recall as the sole proof of practical competence.
- Scoring consistency matters when results affect access, certification, or role readiness.

## Quality gate
The assessment is ready when each objective maps to valid evidence, scoring criteria are explicit and fair, irrelevant barriers are minimized, critical capabilities cannot be masked by averaging, and results lead to specific remediation or demonstrated readiness.
